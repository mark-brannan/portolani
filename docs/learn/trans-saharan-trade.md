# The trans-Saharan trade

In 1352 Ibn Battuta reached a town in the middle of the Sahara where the houses and the mosque were built of salt and roofed with camel skins. Flies swarmed, the water was brackish, and gold dust changed hands by the qintar. That town, Taghaza, was one end of the trade that made Mali rich: salt going south, gold going north, and between them a sea of sand that only a hired guide could cross. This page follows the roads, the goods and the people on them. For the king who ruled the southern end, see [Mali and Mansa Musa](mali-and-mansa-musa.md).

## The roads and the hubs

Camel caravans crossed the Sahara regularly from the 7th and 8th centuries. Salt, cloth, beads, cowries and metal goods went south; gold, slaves, ivory and kola came north. Ibn Battuta says an ordinary caravan had about a thousand camels, and some had twelve thousand.

| Hub | Where | What happened there |
|---|---|---|
| Sijilmasa | Northern edge of the desert, in today's Morocco | The northern end of the western road; Sudanese gold was struck into Almoravid dinars here |
| Taghaza | Deep in the western Sahara | Salt mines; houses of salt |
| Walata | Southern edge of the desert | Where the road met Mali's officials, and where Ibn Battuta first met them |
| Tuat, with Tamentit | Oases of the central Sahara | Date groves on underground channels; a market where gold was bought and sold |
| Takedda | West of the Aïr, in today's Niger | Copper mines; traders left for Egypt every year |
| Ghadames | On the road to Tripoli | An oasis town and a hub of the slave trade |

Other roads ran from Tripoli to Lake Chad, and the Forty Days Road ran up to Egypt. A Mali pilgrim bound for Cairo could go east by Takedda, whose merchants already made the trip each year.

## Salt

At Taghaza, Ibn Battuta wrote, "no one lives at Taghaza except the slaves of the Massufa tribe, who dig for the salt". A camel carried two slabs. In Mali, salt was not just a good but a currency:

> The negroes use salt as a medium of exchange, just as gold and silver is used elsewhere; they cut it up into pieces.

That is Ibn Battuta, in H. A. R. Gibb's translation. A load that sold for 8 to 10 mithqals of gold at Walata fetched 20 to 30, sometimes 40, in Mali.

Taghaza did not last. In 1586 a Moroccan force of 200 musketeers took it, and the miners fled to Taoudenni, about 150 km to the south-east. Our only early source for that move is the Timbuktu chronicler al-Sa'di, writing in the 17th century. Taoudenni is still worked. In the 20th century its slabs weighed about 30 kg, and caravans of more than 4,000 camels left Timbuktu to fetch them.

## The guide and his dangers

From Sijilmasa to Walata took Ibn Battuta "two months to a day". Beyond Taghaza lay ten nights with almost no water. The caravan's life hung on the *takshif*, a man of the Massufa hired to ride ahead to Walata with letters, so that water could be sent out to meet them. Ibn Battuta's party paid him 100 mithqals of gold. Many a takshif never arrived:

> There is no visible road or track in these parts, nothing but sand blown hither and thither by the wind.

The desert, Ibn Battuta adds, is haunted by demons that make sport of a man alone and disorder his mind. One straggler from his own caravan was found dead under a shrub.

## Gold

The gold came from the south: Bambuk, between the Falémé and the Senegal, and Bure, on the upper Niger in today's Guinea. It travelled north as dust, bars and blanks, and at Sijilmasa became coin.

Mali was not the first kingdom to grow rich on it. In 1068 the geographer al-Bakri, writing in Córdoba from what travellers told him, described Ghana, the gold kingdom before Mali. Its capital was two towns six miles apart, one Muslim, one the king's. Ghana taxed what passed through:

| Goods | Tax |
|---|---|
| A donkey-load of salt coming in | One dinar |
| A donkey-load of salt going out | Two dinars |
| A load of copper | Five mithqals |
| Other goods | Ten mithqals |

And the king kept the best of the gold: "The nuggets found in all the mines of his country are reserved for the king, only the gold dust being left for the people." A later geographer, al-Idrisi, said a Ghana king owned a single block of gold of about 13.5 kg.

## The people carried north

Enslaved people were part of this trade from Roman times to the 20th century. The historian John Wright estimates about 5,000 a year over some 1,250 years, between six and seven million people in all, most of them after the 16th century. Enslaved women and men, soldiers among them, served in Egypt and Morocco. At Taghaza the salt itself was cut by slaves. When Ibn Battuta left Takedda in 1353 to go home, his caravan carried 600 enslaved women.

## The silent trade

The oldest story about African gold is a story of trade without words. Herodotus, in the 5th century BC, repeats what the Carthaginians told him. Beyond the Pillars of Heracles they laid their goods on a beach, lit a smoky fire and went back to their ships. The locals came, set gold beside the goods and withdrew. Neither side touched the other's goods until both were satisfied.

Two thousand years later the Venetian Alvise Cadamosto heard much the same of the gold people further south, the Wangara, from traders in 1455. Retellings put the scene on the Niger, with drums for signals; nobody cites a source for that. Paulo de Moraes Farias, writing in 1974, doubted the whole thing; Thurstan Shaw thought it "almost certainly" based on fact. Ibn Battuta, who was there, does not mention it.

## A Genoese at the gold market

In 1447 Antonio Malfante, an agent of a Genoese bank, reached the oasis of Tamentit in Tuat and wrote a letter home in Latin. He found a market where gold was bought and sold, most people living on dates, and Jewish merchants holding much of the trade. He never went further south. What he wrote of the Niger he had from others: that it passed "the gates of Thambet", Timbuktu, and that many boats traded on it.

## How firm it is

| Claim | Where it comes from | How firm |
|---|---|---|
| Salt houses at Taghaza, slaves cutting the salt | Ibn Battuta, who stayed there | Eyewitness |
| Salt used as money in Mali | Ibn Battuta | Eyewitness |
| The takshif paid 100 mithqals | Ibn Battuta | Eyewitness, one caravan |
| Taghaza abandoned for Taoudenni in 1586 | Al-Sa'di, 17th century | Late, but the only source and widely followed |
| Ghana's taxes and the king's nuggets | Al-Bakri, 1068, from informants | Reported; no evidence gold itself was taxed |
| A 13.5 kg block of gold | Al-Idrisi, 12th century | Reported, one source |
| Six to seven million people carried north | Wright's estimate | A modern estimate |
| The silent trade | Herodotus; Cadamosto's informants | Legend; disputed since 1974 |
| West Africa supplied two-thirds of the Mediterranean's gold | Popular articles | Unsourced; no estimator named |
| Malfante at Tamentit | His letter | Reported; his Niger is hearsay |
