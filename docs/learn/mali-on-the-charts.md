# Mali on the charts

On the Catalan Atlas a single sentence sits beside a small painted ship off the coast of Africa. It says a Majorcan sailor set out for the River of Gold on 10 August 1346. It does not say what happened to him, and no record does. Between that ship and the African king enthroned inland from it lies the story of this page: how European chartmakers learned of Mali's gold, drew it, and then sent ships to find it. For the atlas and its maker, see [charts and makers](charts-and-makers.md); for the king, see [Mali and Mansa Musa](mali-and-mansa-musa.md).

## Dulcert, 1339

Angelino Dulcert's chart of 1339, made in Majorca, is the earliest surviving dated Catalan chart. It reaches down the Atlantic coast of Africa past the Canaries and puts human figures in the Sahara. It is credited with the first picture of the king of Mali, labelled *Rex Melly*. That rests on later descriptions of the chart; the label has not been checked here against the chart itself.

## The Catalan Atlas, about 1375

Cresques Abraham's atlas, made in Majorca and in the French royal library by 1380, gives Mali its most famous portrait. On the Africa sheet, at the western edge of the Sahara, Musa sits on a throne in a gold crown, a sceptre in one hand and a round gold nugget or orb held up in the other. The legend beside him reads, in Wikipedia's translation:

> This black Lord is called Musse Melly and is the sovereign of the land of the black people of Gineva. This king is the richest and noblest of all these lands due to the abundance of gold that is extracted from his lands.

"Musse Melly" fuses Musa and Mali into one name. "Gineva" is the old name for the lands of the Black peoples south of the Sahara; Wikipedia glosses it as Ghana.

North of him the desert is full of people. A caption over the Saharan country says:

> All this land is populated by people who cover themselves such that only their eyes can be seen; they live in tents and ride in camels.

These are the veiled Sanhaja Berbers who guided the caravans. Further east, in the central Sahara, another king sits: "Here rules the King of Organa, a Saracen that constantly battles with the Saracens of the coast and with other Arabs." No medieval kingdom of that name is known. Scholars have proposed Kanem-Bornu, Ouargla, Ghana and Ife; none is settled. The atlas also marks a "city of Melly".

## Jaume Ferrer's boat, 1346

Off the coast, below the Canaries, a bright ship flies the flag of Aragon. Its legend, in Catalan:

> Partic l'uixer d'en Jacme Ferrer per 'nar al Riu de l'Or al jorn de Sent Llorenç qui és a X d'agost e fo en l'any MCCCXLVI.

"The uxer of Jacme Ferrer left to go to the River of Gold on St Lawrence's day, which is 10 August, in the year 1346." An uxer was a one-masted cargo galley. The atlas says no more. A garbled Latin note found in the Genoese archives in 1802 says a Catalan galley left Majorca for a river called "Vedamel", and that nothing was ever heard of it. Vedamel may be Arabic *wad al-mal*, "river of wealth". If Ferrer got past Cape Bojador and lived, he was there some ninety years before the Portuguese.

## The figure travels

The enthroned African king became a stock figure. Mecia de Viladestes's chart of 1413 pictures African rulers, among them a "Rex Musameli". Jacopo Russo's map of 1525 shows an African monarch selling gold to merchants, and Diogo Homem's Queen Mary Atlas of 1558 shows an "Emperor of Mali". On a map made at Ancona by Conte di Ottomanno Freducci, about 1529, the king of Mali is drawn as a white, European-style king, with the words "he has great riches". By then the painters had forgotten the man and kept the gold.

## The River of Gold that wasn't

In 1434 Gil Eanes took a Portuguese ship past Cape Bojador. Two years later Afonso Gonçalves Baldaia sailed further south and found an inlet he named Rio do Ouro, "imagining it was the mouth of the legendary 'River of Gold' spoken of by Trans-Saharan traders". He took two horses aboard in case he had to give chase. It was a bay, not a river, and it held no gold. His crew hunted monk seals instead and came home with a cargo of seal pelts and oil. Prince Henry, disappointed, sent no one for some years.

In 1445 the Portuguese set up a trading post at Arguin, further down the coast. It bought gum arabic and slaves; by 1455 about 800 captives a year went from there to Portugal.

## Cadamosto and Gomes

The Venetian Alvise Cadamosto sailed for Henry in 1455 and 1456. He heard that Berber caravans carried salt from Taghaza to Timbuktu, and that Mali's gold left in three parts: one east to Egypt, one north to Tunisia, one west to Morocco. Part of the western share, he said, was turned aside to the Portuguese at Arguin. That last claim was his own, and a hope as much as a measurement.

In 1456 Diogo Gomes sailed about fifty leagues up the Gambia to Cantor, a market in the Mali gold trade, won over a chief he called Numimansa, and gathered news of gold mines, Timbuktu and the roads to Morocco. Gomes told this decades later, in old age, to Martin Behaim. Self-promotion, a long memory, interpreters and poor Latin all stand between us and what he saw. His account holds details found nowhere else, and none of them can be checked.

## Envoys and a castle

In 1482 the Portuguese built São Jorge da Mina, Elmina, on the Gold Coast, from stone cut in Portugal and shipped out ready to raise. In the early 16th century about 24,000 ounces of gold a year passed through it. By sea, Europeans could now buy gold without African middlemen.

Mali reached back. In 1487 Mansa Mahmud received two Portuguese envoys, Pêro d'Évora and Gonçalo Enes. In 1493 he sent an envoy of his own to propose an alliance against a Fula war leader, Tenguella. It came to nothing.

## Books worth more than gold

About 1510 a young diplomat from Fez, al-Hasan al-Wazzan, later called Leo Africanus, visited Timbuktu, by then a Songhai city. His *Description of Africa* was written in Italy in 1526 and put into English by John Pory in 1600:

> Hither are brought divers manuscripts or written bookes out of Barbarie, which are sold for more money than any other merchandize.

Leo wrote from memory, and his book was long Europe's leading authority on Africa.

## Tondibi, 1591

The gold story ends with an invasion. In 1590 Ahmad al-Mansur, sultan of Morocco, sent Judar Pasha across the Sahara with 8,000 camels, about 4,000 fighting men, many armed with arquebuses, and six or eight cannon. They raided the Taghaza salt mines on the way and met the far larger Songhai army at Tondibi, near Gao, on 13 March 1591. The Songhai drove a herd of cattle at the Moroccan lines. The guns stampeded the cattle back into the Songhai ranks, and the battle was over in about two hours. Al-Mansur had believed Songhai held gold mines. It did not; the gold came from fields further south that Songhai only taxed.

| Musa on the Catalan Atlas, with nugget or orb | The atlas | Firm; translations of the legend differ |
| Taghaza and Timbuktu labelled on the atlas | Retellings | Not confirmed in any source cited here |
| The King of Organa | The atlas | The label is firm; who he was is a guess |
