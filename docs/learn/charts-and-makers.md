# The charts and makers behind the ornaments

Each ornament on this map is drawn after, or cut from, a named chart. The drawer's attribution lists them. This page says what is known of the ones whose sources have been checked.

## Carte Pisane, about 1275 to 1300

The oldest surviving nautical chart. It is conventionally thought Genoese; the place it was found, Pisa, gave it its name. It carries hazard symbols in black only, the cross for rocks among them.

## Catalan Atlas, about 1375

A world map made by Cresques Abraham, a Jewish cartographer of Palma, Majorca, with his son Jehuda. It is dated 1375 by convention; it was probably made in the late 1370s or early 1380s. It was in the French royal library by 1380 and is in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It draws many cities as small pictures, a cross on Christian cities and a dome on others, each with a flag. Its western part is like a portolan chart, and it is the first known chart to put a compass rose at the centre of a network of rhumb lines.

It names the realms of its day and draws their rulers: Musse Melly, Mansa Musa of Mali, holding a nugget of gold; Jambech, lord of Sarra, the Golden Horde; the sultan of Babylon, as it calls Mamluk Egypt; the king of Tauris in Persia, the sultan of Delhi, and the Great Khan of Catayo. The map's region names of 1375 are these, in the atlas's spelling or read in English.

## Carta Marina, 1539

Olaus Magnus, a Swedish clergyman, made it in Rome between 1527 and 1539. It is the first map of the Nordic countries with detail and place names, 1.70 m by 1.25 m, printed from nine woodcut blocks. The sea is filled with animals, whales and walrus among them. All the original copies dropped out of sight after 1574; one was found in Munich in 1886 and another in Switzerland in 1961. It is a printed map of the north, not a navigator's chart.

## Cartouche

A cartouche is an oval or oblong design edged with ornamental scrollwork, used to hold a title or a picture. As a scrolling frame it dates from the early 16th century, and it stayed in use through the Renaissance and Baroque styles that followed.

## Others the drawer names

The drawer also credits Benincasa (1466), Beccari (1403), Oliva (c. 1590), Aguiar (1492), Martines (1584), Prunes (1559) and a Genoese chart (c. 1320 to 1350). Their entries are not written yet: no source for them has been checked, so this page says nothing about them.
