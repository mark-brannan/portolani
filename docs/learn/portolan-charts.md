# What a portolan chart was

A portolan chart is a nautical chart drawn in ink on vellum. The oldest known examples date from the late 13th and early 14th centuries and show the Mediterranean region, from the Atlantic coasts of western Europe and north-west Africa in the west to the Black Sea in the east.

You can tell one at a glance by four things: it is about the coast, with the inland left nearly blank; colour-coded straight lines run out from one or more centres in 32 directions; a linear scale bar is marked in portolan miles (*miglio*); and place names are written at right angles to the coast.

The word comes from the Italian *portolano*, a book of sailing directions. The name "portolan chart" was coined in the 1890s, on the assumption that the charts belonged with those books. Other names proposed since include rhumb line charts, compass charts and loxodromic charts.

The oldest surviving one is the Carte Pisane, probably made about 1275 to 1300 and now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It shows the whole Mediterranean and the Black Sea, with the best accuracy in the Mediterranean, and says nothing of the inland.

Their most puzzling feature is how good the coastlines are. The oldest known charts are already highly developed, and later charts did not become more accurate over time.

## What they were for

Many historians think mariners used them. Some scholars hold that many, perhaps most, were made as display pieces to show the owner's worldliness, and note that some documented owners had no link to seafaring. Charts used aboard ship mostly do not survive.
