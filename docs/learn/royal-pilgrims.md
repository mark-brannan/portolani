# Royal pilgrims

Mansa Musa was not the first West African king to go to Mecca, nor the last. One before him was murdered on the Libyan coast on his way home. One king of Kanem, a chronicle says, was drowned at Suez by Egyptians who feared him. One after him came back a caliph. Musa's hajj is the famous one because of the gold, but he walked a road his neighbours knew well. For the hajj itself, see [Mali and Mansa Musa](mali-and-mansa-musa.md).

## Mali's pilgrim kings before Musa

**Mansa Uli**, a son of Sunjata, made the hajj sometime in the reign of the Mamluk sultan Baybars, between 1260 and 1277. That is all: Ibn Khaldun records the trip, and nothing about the journey survives. The historian Nehemia Levtzion argued that a king could only cross the desert to Mecca once he held Timbuktu, and perhaps Gao.

**Sakura** was a stranger story. He seized the throne about 1285. Ibn Khaldun calls him a *mawla* of the royal house, a freedman or client; oral tradition calls him *jonni*, "little slave", though he may have come from free clans that were called slaves only as a figure of speech. He went to Mecca in the reign of al-Nasir Muhammad, the same sultan Musa met, and was killed on the way home at Tajura, about 12 miles east of Tripoli. Ibn Khaldun does not say who killed him. Griot tradition says it was Kon Mamadi, a grandson of Sunjata, helped by Sakura's own daughter.

## Kanem and the sunken king

East of Mali, the kings of Kanem, near Lake Chad, also went to Mecca by way of Cairo. Their royal chronicle, the *girgam*, written down much later, says the mai Hummay died in Egypt on pilgrimage. His son Dunama made the hajj twice, each time bringing 300 slaves to sell in Cairo. On the second trip, the chronicle says, the Egyptians feared he meant to conquer them. At Suez they opened a cock in his ship's hull and sank it. His followers saw him "floating in the sea in his white pilgrimage garments" until he went down.

Kanem's pilgrims left more lasting marks in Cairo. About 1242 a madrasa was built there to house them, paid for by the West African community or, more likely, by the ruler of Bornu. By the mid-13th century al-Azhar had a residence for students from Bornu.

## The Takruri in Cairo

To the Mamluks, "Takrur" meant the Muslim lands of the Sahel from the Atlantic to Lake Chad, and a West African in Cairo was "al-Takruri". About twenty Takruri Sufi saints of the 13th to 15th centuries are buried and venerated in Cairo's great cemetery, the Qarafa, where Musa himself lodged in 1324. Later, West Africans kept hostels in Jerusalem and Medina. When Musa arrived he joined a community, not a void.

## The road

From Cairo the Mamluk pilgrim road ran east through Sinai to Ayla, at the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, then south through Arabia. Pilgrims camped for three or four days at the pool of Birkat al-Hajj outside Cairo before setting out, and the whole journey to Mecca took one to one and a half months.

The Sahara half is harder. No source gives Musa's stages between Mali and Cairo. He may well have gone by Walata and the oases, as the trade did, but that is a guess, not a record. The best measure of the desert is Ibn Battuta's, on a different journey in 1352: two months from Sijilmasa to Walata. The road he took is on [the trans-Saharan trade](trans-saharan-trade.md).

Modern maps disagree, which shows how open the question is. One reproduced by [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/the-past/mansa-musa-mali-king-atlantic/) runs Musa north from the capital past Timbuktu to the salt mines of Taghaza, then due east across the open Sahara to Cairo. The line on this chart goes by Walata and the oases of Tuat, and comes home by Gao and Timbuktu. [Gabriel Moss's map of the empire in 1337](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_Mali_Empire.jpg) draws no hajj at all, only the trade roads from Walata, Timbuktu and Gao to Morocco, Tunis and Tripoli. Each follows the trade roads; none records Musa's.

## Mecca, 1324

Only one writer saw Musa at Mecca. The Meccan scholar al-Yafi'i says he "presented himself for the pilgrimage with thousands of his soldiers", heavily armed. Al-Yafi'i also records a fight inside the sanctuary between Malian and Turkic pilgrims, and says Musa persuaded his men to stand down. The way home went badly: travelling apart from the main caravan, many of the Malians died of hunger or at the hands of bandits before they reached Suez.

On that trip Musa met the Andalusi poet and jurist Abu Ishaq al-Sahili and persuaded him to come to Mali. Musa later paid him 12,000 mithqals of gold for a royal audience chamber.

## Askia Muhammad, the bookkeeper's hajj

In late 1496 the Songhai ruler Askia Muhammad set out from Gao with about 1,500 men, 500 of them mounted. He carried 300,000 gold pieces, taken from the treasury of his predecessor, and split them exactly: 100,000 in alms at the holy places, 100,000 for a garden in Medina for the people of Takrur, and 100,000 for goods. Then, the Timbuktu chronicle says, he asked the Abbasid caliph to make him his deputy in Songhai. The caliph told him to give up his rule for three days, and then installed him with a cap and a turban.

> The Askiya became a true Islamic caliph.

That is the *Tarikh al-Sudan*, written about 1655, in an English version whose translator the online text does not name. Askia came home in 1497 or 1498, by some accounts owing 50,000 ducats.

## The legends of Musa's road

Some of the best-known stories about Musa's pilgrimage have no source anyone has found.

- **A mosque every Friday.** He is said to have built a mosque wherever he stopped on a Friday. Wikipedia credits it to a 1972 book by Bell and says "reportedly"; no primary source for it has been found.
- **Gold thrown to the crowds.** The sources say he *gave* gold to the poor and to the cities he passed, Cairo and Medina among them. Throwing it is a later picture.
- **Atonement.** A 17th-century chronicle, reworked in the 19th century, says Musa accidentally killed his mother before the hajj. No source read links that to the pilgrimage as penance; the link is a modern guess.
