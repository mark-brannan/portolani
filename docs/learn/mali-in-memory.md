# Mali in memory

Ask a griot in the old Mande heartland about Mali's greatest king and you will hear of Sunjata, the lame boy who rose on an iron staff and beat a sorcerer. You will hear much less of Mansa Musa, and some griots will tell you he spent Mali's wealth. The outside world remembers it the other way round. This page is about how Mali has been remembered: by its singers, by a traveller who laughed at its king, by archaeologists who cannot find its capital, and by the internet. For the history itself, start at [Mali and Mansa Musa](mali-and-mansa-musa.md).

## Sunjata and the griots

The epic of Sunjata is sung by Maninka griots, *jeli*, and has passed by mouth from generation to generation. There is no single text; a full telling can run past forty episodes. In outline:

- Sunjata is the son of a king and of Sogolon, an ugly, hunchbacked wife. As a boy he cannot walk and is mocked for it.
- One day he pulls himself up on an iron staff (in other tellings a baobab branch) and grows as strong as a lion.
- His half-brother and the half-brother's mother drive Sunjata and Sogolon into exile.
- Meanwhile Soumaoro Kanté, the sorcerer-king of Sosso, conquers nine kingdoms. His magic balafon is stolen by Balla Fasséké, Sunjata's griot.
- Sunjata returns with an army, defeats Soumaoro at Kirina and takes the title *mansa*.

The version most readers know comes from the griot Mamadou Kouyaté, put into French by D. T. Niane around 1960 and into English by G. D. Pickett in 1965. Niane's text reads like a novel. Historians use the epic carefully: it has no dates, and the Islamic touches in some versions look like later additions. Sunjata himself is real; Ibn Khaldun and Ibn Battuta, writing about a century on, confirm he lived. The battle of Kirina is usually put about 1235, but that year is a later reckoning.

## The charter of Kouroukan Fouga

After Kirina, tradition says, Sunjata's assembly at Kouroukan Fouga proclaimed a charter for the new empire. UNESCO listed "the Manden Charter, proclaimed in Kurukan Fuga" as intangible heritage in 2009. The text we have was put together at a workshop at Kankan, in Guinea, in 1998, under Siriman Kouyaté, with ten traditional communicators. It has 44 articles. Among them:

- "Everybody has a right to life and to the preservation of physical integrity."
- "Never offend women, our mothers."
- "To satisfy one's hunger is not robbery if you don't take away anything in your bag or your pocket."

Those are lines of the 1998 text. Whether they go back to the 13th century is disputed. Nick Nesbitt reads the charter as a statement of human rights older than Europe's. Jan Jansen calls it "bricolage" and recent invention; Francis Simonis notes that no Malian griots were invited to the workshop and that the text gives a large place to the Kouyaté family.

## Ibn Battuta at court

Ibn Battuta spent eight months in Mali's capital in 1352 and 1353, in the reign of Musa's brother Sulayman. He is the only outside eyewitness to the court, and he did not hold back. All the quotations here are from H. A. R. Gibb's 1929 translation.

He waited for the king's welcome gift. A friend ran out barefoot to tell him it had come.

> It was three cakes of bread, and a piece of beef fried in native oil, and a calabash of sour curds. When I saw this I burst out laughing, and thought it a most amazing thing that they could be so foolish and make so much of such a paltry matter.

He judged Sulayman "a miserly king, not a man from whom one might hope for a rich present". At audiences, a man the king answered would bare his back and throw dust over his head, "for all the world like a bather splashing himself with water. I used to wonder how it was they did not blind themselves."

On feast days the poets came, each dressed in a figure of feathers like a thrush, with a wooden head and a red beak. "They stand in front of the sultan in this ridiculous make-up and recite their poems." Their verses reminded the king of the kings who had sat on his platform before him, and told him to do good deeds that would outlive him. Ibn Battuta was told the custom was older than Islam there.

He had a good word too, and it is the one Mali's admirers quote:

> There is complete security in their country. Neither traveller nor inhabitant in it has anything to fear from robbers or men of violence.

## The capital nobody has found

Al-Umari named Mali's capital "Nyeni". Since the 1920s it has been placed at Niani, on the Sankarani river in Guinea, and Guinean and Polish teams dug there in 1965 and 1968. They found houses, stone foundations and a mihrab. In 2012 François-Xavier Fauvelle reviewed the evidence in a paper called "Niani Redux". He found that the identification had been built on grounds that had little to do with the digs, and that "there are no arguments in favour of this hypothesis". Most historians now agree. Other candidates include Dakajalan, Kangaba and Manikoro, and some argue Mali had no fixed capital at all, only a court that went where the mansa went.

## Sankore, which was not a university

Timbuktu's mosques of Djinguereber, Sidi Yahya and Sankore were centres of learning. Sankore is often called a university, but there is no sign of one: no central administration, no register of students, no set course. Scholars taught their own pupils, who paid them. One of them, Ahmad Baba, wrote over forty books. After the Moroccan conquest of 1591 he was arrested, exiled and lost his library of about 1,600 volumes.

## The manuscripts

Timbuktu's manuscripts date from the late 13th to the early 20th century, in Arabic and in African languages written in Arabic script, on law, medicine, science and the Qur'an. A figure of 700,000 has circulated since at least 2006. It is an estimate; nobody has counted them all.

In 2012 armed Islamist groups took Timbuktu. Abdel Kader Haidara, curator of one of the city's great family libraries, and the preservation expert Stephanie Diakité organised a rescue. Local families hid the books in their homes, and from there they were carried to Bamako. Some 350,000 manuscripts reached safety; about 300,000 were still in Bamako in 2022. About 4,203 were burned or stolen.

## The richest man ever

In 2012 the website Celebrity Net Worth ranked the richest people in history and put Musa first, at the equivalent of $400 billion. The Atlanta Black Star carried it on 16 October that year, and the claim has run round the internet ever since. Historians object. Hadrien Collet, Michael Gomez and the journalist Jacob Davidson all say that turning medieval reports of gold into modern dollars cannot be done soundly. The big tonnages come from one chain of writers, not several, and much of the gold on the hajj may not have been the king's own. What the sources show is that people were amazed, which is a different thing from a balance sheet.

## Musa in a game

In *Civilization VI* Mansa Musa leads Mali, added in the Gathering Storm expansion. His ability, "Sahel Merchants", earns gold from trade routes for each desert tile around the home city. A king some of his own griots remember as a spendthrift is, in the game, a merchant.

## How firm it is

| Claim | Where it comes from | How firm |
|---|---|---|
| Sunjata's childhood, the staff, the sorcerer | The epic, sung by griots | Legend |
| Sunjata lived and founded Mali | Ibn Khaldun, Ibn Battuta | Firm |
| Kirina in about 1235 | A later reckoning | The battle firm, the year not |
| The Kouroukan Fouga charter | A 1998 reconstruction, listed by UNESCO in 2009 | Its age contested |
| The bread, the dust, the bird poets, the safe roads | Ibn Battuta, eyewitness | Firm, in his words |
| Niani as the capital | 20th-century identification | Doubted; "no arguments in favour" |
| A University of Sankore | A modern label | Wrong as a label; the learning was real |
| 700,000 manuscripts | An estimate since 2006 | A guess |
| 350,000 rescued in 2012 to 2013 | The rescuers' count | Reported |
| Richest person ever, $400 billion | Celebrity Net Worth, 2012 | Unsound |
