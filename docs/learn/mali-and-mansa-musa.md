# Mali and Mansa Musa

In 1324 a king from south of the Sahara rode into Cairo with a caravan so large, and gave away so much gold, that people wrote about it for a century. Before then Mali was a name few outside West Africa knew. Within fifteen years it was on European charts, and within fifty its king sat enthroned on the Catalan Atlas, holding up a gold nugget or orb. This page says what the sources tell of Mansa Musa, the trade he ruled, and the fleet his predecessor is said to have sailed into the Atlantic.

## The king and the trade

Mansa Musa ruled Mali from about 1312 to about 1337. The kingdom reached across parts of today's Mali, Senegal, the Gambia, Guinea, Niger, Mauritania and Burkina Faso. It was already rich when he came to power: its wealth came less from mining than from taxing and controlling trade. Gold came from Bambuk and Bure in the south and was traded north; salt came south from the Sahara, above all from Taghaza; copper was taxed too and changed hands for gold. Ivory was another source of wealth.

Musa's work was to connect. Gao and Timbuktu on the Niger came under Mali in his reign, though how firmly is debated. When the Mossi took Timbuktu in 1330 he took it back, walled it and garrisoned it. He brought scholars and builders from across the Islamic world, and Arab merchants followed the news of his wealth. Timbuktu grew into a centre of Islamic learning; the Djinguereber Mosque there is dated to 1327.

## The hajj, 1324 to 1325

Musa went on pilgrimage to Mecca by way of Cairo. He camped by the pyramids for three days, entered the city on 19 July 1324, stayed about three months, and left with the official caravan on 18 October. His host was the emir Ibn Amir Hajib, and almost everything we know of the visit comes from what that emir later told the scholar al-Umari, who wrote it down about 1337 to 1338, some thirteen years after.

For days Musa would not go up to the Citadel to meet the sultan, al-Nasir Muhammad. The emir worked out why: he would have to kiss the ground and the sultan's hand. Musa had told him he came "for the Pilgrimage and nothing else". When at last he stood before the sultan, the court told him to kiss the ground.

> But he refused outright saying: "How may this be?" Then an intelligent man who was with him whispered to him something we could not understand and he said: "I make obeisance to God who created me!" then he prostrated himself and went forward to the sultan. The sultan half rose to greet him and sat him by his side.

That is al-Umari, in the translation of Levtzion and Hopkins. The two kings talked for a long time, and the sultan sent him robes of honour and saddled horses.

Then the gold. Al-Umari says Musa left "no court emir nor holder of a royal office without the gift of a load of gold".

> They exchanged gold until they depressed its value in Egypt and caused its price to fall.

Before Musa came, al-Umari says, a mithqal of gold did not go below 25 dirhams; since then it "does not exceed 22 dirhams or less", and had stayed so for about twelve years when he wrote.

By the end of his stay in Cairo Musa had run out. He sold property and borrowed from the city's merchants at high interest, before the long road home. Many lenders were not fully repaid; other sources say some were. One of them, Siraj al-Din ibn al-Kuwayk of Alexandria, was owed 50,000 dinars by Ibn Khaldun's account. In 1334 he crossed the Sahara to Mali to collect, stayed as the guest of the poet al-Sahili, and died there. Foul play was suspected; his son said he died naturally. Musa had already died himself.

Mecca, the road, and the West African kings who walked it before and after him are on [royal pilgrims](royal-pilgrims.md).

## A story told in Mali

Ibn Battuta, who visited Mali in 1352 to 1353, heard a story about the hajj from a governor, Farba Magha, who had been on it. A white qadi in Musa's party tried to make off with 4,000 mithqals. Musa sent him into exile among the heathen cannibals. He lived there four years and came back uneaten.

> The reason why the heathens did not eat him was that he was white, for they say that the white is indigestible because he is not "ripe".

That is Ibn Battuta in H. A. R. Gibb's translation: a traveller's tale, told to him second-hand.

## How the numbers grew

The famous size of Musa's party grew the further its writers stood from 1324.

| Writer | When | What he says of the party |
|---|---|---|
| Al-Yafi'i, in Mecca | Mid-14th century; the only eyewitness | "Thousands of his soldiers", armed |
| Al-Umari | About 1337 to 1338 | "Companions" and a "suite"; the only slaves he names are Turkish ones the sultan gave Musa |
| Ibn al-Wardi | Late 1340s | More than 10,000 Takruri, West African Muslims |
| Ibn Kathir | 1366 | About 20,000 "servants", the first time the word appears |
| Al-Qalqashandi | 1412 | "It is said" 12,000 maids carried his effects |
| Al-Maqrizi | Before 1442 | "It is said" 14,000 maids |
| Tarikh al-Sudan, Timbuktu | About 1655 | 60,000 soldiers, with 500 slaves carrying gold wands |

Historians read the crowd differently. Michael Gomez holds that most of the retinue was enslaved. John Hunwick calls the figures "grossly inflated", since the Sahara could not water such a host. Isaac Samuel argues the earliest writers describe free, armed companions. Enslaved people were surely among them; nobody counted.

## The numbers, and how firm they are

| Claim | Where it comes from | How firm |
|---|---|---|
| Reign c. 1312 to c. 1337 | Accession dated 25 years before his death | Most accepted; 1332 is also given for his death |
| In Cairo 19 July to 18 October 1324 | Wikipedia, citing Levtzion and Hopkins | One chain; treat the days as approximate |
| Gold fell from at least 25 to at most 22 dirhams a mithqal | Al-Umari, about 12 years on | His report; Schultz finds it a normal Mamluk swing |
| The party's size | Seven writers over three centuries, above | Grows with distance; the eyewitness gives no count |
| 80 camels each carrying 23 to 136 kg of gold dust | Popular retellings | No named medieval source traced |
| About 18 tonnes of gold | One chain, al-Umari to Ibn Khaldun | Not independent; one source repeated |
| Many lenders not fully repaid | Al-Umari, Ibn Khaldun; others disagree | Reported; Siraj al-Din's agents recovered some |
| Richest person ever, $400 billion | A celebrity website, 2012 | Unsound; Collet argues it cannot be calculated |
| The poet al-Sahili designed the Djinguereber Mosque | Long tradition | Doubted; only a royal audience chamber is firmly his |

## The fleet before Musa

In Cairo Musa told Ibn Amir Hajib how he came to rule. "We belong to a house which hands on the kingship by inheritance," he began. His predecessor believed the far edge of the Atlantic could be found. He fitted out 200 ships with men and 200 with gold, water and food, and told them not to return until they reached the end of the ocean or ran out of supplies. One ship came back; its captain said that out at sea a river with a strong current had carried the rest away. The king did not believe him. He fitted out 2,000 ships, 1,000 for himself and his men and 1,000 for supplies, left Musa as his deputy, and sailed. None came back, and Musa became king.

Al-Umari wrote this down from Ibn Amir Hajib. It is the only account: no other medieval Arabic writer and no West African tradition tells it.

- **Who was he?** The voyage is often credited to "Abu Bakr II", but that name comes from a 19th-century mistranslation of Ibn Khaldun, found out by Levtzion in 1963. Ibn Khaldun names Musa's predecessor as Muhammad ibn Qu; some historians suggest his father, Qu.
- **The river.** Most read it as the Canary Current, which runs south along the coast and then west towards the Americas. John Thornton takes it as a sign the court understood the ocean.
- **Did it reach the Americas?** Ivan van Sertima and Gaoussou Diawara have argued so. No African artefact has been found in a controlled excavation in the New World, and historians reject the crossing. Robin Law reads the story the other way, as a sign of how little West Africa then knew of the open sea, though coastal trade ran between Cape Mount and Cape Verde. The sail and rudder for working against the current came later, with the Portuguese.
- **Why tell it?** François-Xavier Fauvelle reads Musa's point as how the failed voyage made him king. Some suspect the story covers how he secured the throne, and that he let his predecessor's memory fade. Oral tradition says almost nothing of either man; it remembers Sunjata, the founder, far more than Musa, and some griots fault Musa for spending Mali's wealth.

On this map the fleet's leg is drawn as doubted, from the Senegal mouth, where al-Umari puts Mali's coast, out into open sea. Where it ended no one knows.

## On the charts

The hajj is why Mali entered the old world's view. Angelino Dulcert's chart of 1339 is credited with the first such figure, labelled *Rex Melly*, the king of Mali. The Catalan Atlas of about 1375 draws him on a throne with a sceptre and a gold nugget or orb, the figure this map's overlay shows. [Mali on the charts](mali-on-the-charts.md) follows the figure from chart to chart, and [charts and makers](charts-and-makers.md) describes the atlas.

## After Musa

Mali outlived him. Ibn Battuta visited in the reign of Musa's brother Sulayman, whom he called "a miserly king". Civil war and weak rule broke Mali apart in the 1460s, and Songhai rose to the east. A smaller Mali held on in the west until the Bamana sacked its capital in the 17th century. Where that capital stood is itself unsettled; see [Mali in memory](mali-in-memory.md).
