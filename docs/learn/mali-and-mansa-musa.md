# Mali and Mansa Musa

In 1324 a king from south of the Sahara rode into Cairo with a caravan so large, and gave away so much gold, that people wrote about it for a century. Before then Mali was a name few outside West Africa knew. Within fifteen years it was on European charts, and within fifty its king sat enthroned on the Catalan Atlas, holding up a nugget of gold. This page says what the sources tell of Mansa Musa, the trade he ruled, and the fleet his predecessor is said to have sailed into the Atlantic.

## The king and the trade

Mansa Musa ruled Mali from about 1312 to about 1337. The kingdom reached across parts of today's Mali, Senegal, the Gambia, Guinea, Niger, Mauritania and Burkina Faso. It was already rich when he came to power: its wealth came less from mining than from taxing and controlling trade. Gold came from Bambuk and Bure in the south and was traded north; salt came south from the Sahara, above all from Taghaza; copper was taxed too and changed hands for gold. Ivory was another source of wealth.

Musa's work was to connect. Gao and Timbuktu on the Niger came under Mali in his reign, though how firmly is debated. When the Mossi took Timbuktu in 1330 he took it back, walled it and garrisoned it. He brought scholars and builders from across the Islamic world, and Arab merchants followed the news of his wealth. Timbuktu grew into a centre of Islamic learning; the Djinguereber Mosque there is dated to 1327.

## The hajj, 1324 to 1325

Musa went on pilgrimage to Mecca by way of Cairo. He camped by the pyramids for three days, entered the city on 19 July 1324, stayed about three months, and left with the official caravan on 18 October. In Cairo he met the Mamluk sultan and spent and gave away gold freely.

The scholar al-Umari, writing a few years later, says a mithqal of gold was worth 25 dirhams before Musa came and fell below 22 after, and did not recover for at least twelve years. On the way home Musa ran short and borrowed from Cairo's merchants at high interest; al-Umari and Ibn Khaldun say the lenders were never fully repaid.

The hajj is why Mali entered the old world's view. A remote kingdom turned out to have a king with tens of thousands of followers and more gold than seemed possible. Angelino Dulcert's chart of 1339 marks him as *Rex Melly*, the king of Mali, and the Catalan Atlas of about 1375 draws him on a throne with a sceptre and a gold nugget, the figure this map's overlay shows. ## The numbers, and how firm they are

The famous figures travel further than their sources.

| Claim | Where it comes from | How firm |
|---|---|---|
| Reign c. 1312 to c. 1337 | Accession dated 25 years before his death | Most accepted; 1332 is also given for his death |
| In Cairo 19 July to 18 October 1324 | Wikipedia; no primary source checked | One source; treat the days as approximate |
| Gold fell from 25 to under 22 dirhams a mithqal for 12 years | Al-Umari | His report; Schultz finds it a normal Mamluk swing |
| 60,000 men, 12,000 slaves each with a gold bar or staff | Later Arabic accounts, repeated in popular retellings | Literary, not a count |
| 80 camels each carrying 23 to 136 kg of gold dust | Later Arabic accounts | An upper-bound figure of speech, not a measurement |
| About 18 tonnes of gold | One chain, al-Umari to Ibn Khaldun | Not independent; one source repeated |
| Richest person ever, $400 billion | Online articles | Unsound; Collet argues it cannot be calculated |
| The poet al-Sahili designed the Djinguereber Mosque | Long tradition | Doubted; only a royal audience chamber is firmly his |

## The fleet before Musa

In Cairo Musa told the governor, Ibn Amir Hajib, how he came to rule. His predecessor believed the far edge of the Atlantic could be found. He fitted out 200 ships with men and 200 with gold, water and food, and told them not to return until they reached the end of the ocean or ran out of supplies. One ship came back; its captain said that out at sea a river with a strong current had carried the rest away. The king did not believe him. He fitted out 2,000 ships, 1,000 for himself and his men and 1,000 for supplies, left Musa as his deputy, and sailed. None came back, and Musa became king.

Al-Umari wrote this down from Ibn Amir Hajib. It is the only account: no other medieval Arabic writer and no West African tradition tells it.

- **Who was he?** The voyage is often credited to "Abu Bakr II", but that name comes from a 19th-century mistranslation of Ibn Khaldun, found out by Levtzion in 1963. Ibn Khaldun names Musa's predecessor as Muhammad ibn Qu; some historians suggest his father, Qu.
- **The river.** Most read it as the Canary Current, which runs south along the coast and then west towards the Americas. John Thornton takes it as a sign the court understood the ocean.
- **Did it reach the Americas?** Ivan van Sertima and Gaoussou Diawara have argued so. No African artefact has been found in a controlled excavation in the New World, and historians reject the crossing. Robin Law reads the story the other way, as a sign of how little West Africa then knew of the open sea, though coastal trade ran between Cape Mount and Cape Verde. The sail and rudder for working against the current came later, with the Portuguese.
- **Why tell it?** François-Xavier Fauvelle reads Musa's point as how the failed voyage made him king. Some suspect the story covers how he secured the throne, and that he let his predecessor's memory fade. Oral tradition says almost nothing of either man; it remembers Sunjata, the founder, far more than Musa, and some griots fault Musa for spending Mali's wealth.

On this map the fleet's leg is drawn as doubted, from the Senegal mouth, where al-Umari puts Mali's coast, out into open sea. Where it ended no one knows.

## After Musa

Mali outlived him. Ibn Battuta visited in the reign of Sulayman. Civil war and weak rule broke it apart in the 1460s, and Songhai rose to the east. A smaller Mali held on in the west until the Bamana sacked its capital in the 17th century.
